# Studio grade

The opt-in path to the best film the budget allows. It adds study before design, choices the person can see and
hear, assets bought where they show, specialist craft passes, and a loop that keeps raising the film until it clears
a stated bar. Nothing in the main method relaxes: every gate, hard limit, truth rule and QC check still applies, and
no money is spent without the person's yes.

## 1. When it applies

Use studio grade when the person asks for it by name, asks for the highest quality or "the best possible", or gives
a budget for premium assets, generation or specialist work. Otherwise run the main steps; nothing here is required
for a good film.

Studio grade is not more of everything. Every element still earns its place, and the extra effort goes into finding
the idea, testing choices with the person, and taking each weak moment to excellent. A film can be quieter and
simpler at studio grade than it was before. Scale the studio to the piece: a short piece or an already approved idea
keeps the core's tailoring ([short pieces](../SKILL.md#short-pieces)), and its concept reopens only if the person asks.

**Who judges what.** You prepare options, build, measure and inspect frames. Every judgement that needs motion at
playback speed or hearing belongs to a person: how a move feels, a voice, the music, the mix on real speakers,
material credibility, where the eye lands. Record each such judgement with who made it, on which version and how they
watched (device, size, sound on or off). Until then it is pending, whatever the measurements say. A deliverable
planned as silent skips the listening judgements, with the reason recorded.

## 2. Open the studio

Settle these before designing picture or sound:

- **The use.** Ask in plain words: "Will you pay to promote this video, including boosted posts? Where will it
  appear, in which countries, for how long, and might you want shorter or translated versions later? If you're not
  sure, say so and I'll price and check those uses separately." Rights not yet known stay unresolved, and no asset is
  described as cleared for them ([assets](assets.md) §2).
- **The material.** Ask for what the person has, not for a polished brief: "Send a short description or demo of what
  the product does and who it helps, what you can back up with evidence, any approved logo, colours, copy,
  screenshots or recordings, where the film will run, and anything we must not show. Links and rough notes are fine;
  I'll list what's missing and ask before uploading anything private."
- **The cap and the plan.** The person sets the spending cap. Fill [budget.csv](../assets/templates/budget.csv): one
  line per purchase or generation batch, with what it buys for the viewer, the gate that needs it, the most it can
  cost over its whole term with tax (`max_cost`), its commitment (one-off, a batch, or a subscription's term), the
  licence it must carry ([assets](assets.md), [generation](generation.md)) and the fallback if it is declined. Before
  each approval, show what the line costs now and over its term, the total committed so far (paid to date plus
  everything still owed, with an approved subscription counted for its full term until its cancellation is
  confirmed), and what remains under the cap. Never exceed the cap, start a new generation batch or renew beyond an
  approved term without asking.
- **The bar.** Agree two or three reference films the finished piece should be able to sit beside. If the person has
  none, do the reference part of the study first (§4, items 1-3) and show short labelled comparisons; agreeing two or
  three of them completes this item. The person never has to find references themselves.
- **Who judges.** Name who approves each gate in §5, who can watch and listen, and whether a viewer from the intended
  audience is available for the bar (§8).
- **What is owed.** Agree which of the studio deliverables in §9 this film needs, in plain words, with their cost and
  storage. Say before starting any added version or licence that changes the estimate.
- **The first reply.** Lead with the film: describe two or three directions so the person can picture each (§4, item
  5), as ideas the study and the sketches will test. Then give the route in plain words (what the person will see and
  decide at each step, and what each step may cost), the questions above, and how the finished film will be judged:
  the six questions in §8, answered by viewers, with rounds on the weakest moment until each is met or the person
  stops.

Done when `budget.csv` holds the cap and the planned lines, the use and the material are recorded with their gaps,
the references are annotated, every gate has an owner, and the deliverables are agreed.

## 3. Lead someone new to video

People without video experience cannot judge prose treatments, timing numbers or technical terms. They can react
precisely to what they see and hear.

- **Show, don't ask.** Put two or three versions in front of the person and ask which they would watch again, and
  why. Never ask them to choose in a vocabulary they do not use.
- **Name each step by what the person will see and decide**, such as "three rough sketches, to choose the story",
  with any trade name in brackets.
- **Say once, at the first gate, what you cannot do:** "I can prepare these options and measure them, but I can't
  watch them at normal speed or hear them. Please play them on the device you'll use, with sound on. Until someone
  has, I'll report them as unwatched and unheard." Where the person cannot judge something critical, such as a mix
  or a voice, suggest someone who can.
- **Present every gate before the final one the same way:** "This is a rough preview, not the final quality. Today,
  please choose ___. Still temporary: ___. This choice affects ___ later; changing it after picture lock means
  redoing ___. Tell me anything that feels wrong, even if you can't say why."
- **Present the final gate as the finished film:** "This is the finished file, version ___. Checked: ___. Not
  checked, or still open: ___. Approving it means ___."
- **Explore widely in private; present a few.** Make more directions than you show. Present two or three, each with
  the one question it answers and its main cost. Record the choice as "We chose ___ because ___, and will judge it by
  ___", and check later rounds against that line.
- **Label what is provisional.** Mark every temp element (music, voice, type, colour, timing) as temp in the playback
  note, with the job its replacement must keep. Judge the replacement against the job, never against the temp.
- **Translate their words into something to show.** These are starting points for a comparison, not rules:

| They say | Often comes from | Show them |
|---|---|---|
| "Make it more premium" | too many elements, light motion, crowded sound | the same moment with half the elements, longer holds and motion that carries weight |
| "It needs more energy" | holds longer than the content needs, soft attacks | a tighter cut on the same music, with attacks on the cuts |
| "It feels cheap" | a stock cliché, mixed easing, default sound | the cliché replaced by the product's own material, one easing family, designed sound |
| "It's too busy" | several moving things, or several sounds competing for attention at once | one moving element at a time; one sound in front at each moment (§7.8) |
| "It feels rushed" | text leaving before it is read; several new things per cut | reading windows honoured; one new thing per cut (§7.4) |
| "It's boring in the middle" | rows where nothing changes for the viewer | the middle with a consequence, a piece of evidence or a reinterpretation added (§7.1) |
| "The music feels wrong" | too active, or the wrong mood | one version changing density only, one changing harmony and timbre only (§7.6) |

- **Find where the peak was felt.** After the sounded pass, ask "At which moment did you feel the most, if
  anywhere?" Record the time beside the planned peak. When they differ, move the peak, strengthen its preparation or
  retime its cue, then test with a fresh viewer. Keep "felt", "liked" and "understood" as separate observations.

## 4. Study before design

A professional brings a frame of reference the person cannot supply. Build one for this film before designing
picture or sound, and draw the directions from it: an idea you had before the study enters it as a hypothesis for the
references to test.

1. Find comparable pieces (same kind of product, audience or structure) whose makers documented their decisions:
   interviews, breakdowns, case studies. Start with three, and add more, up to eight, only while another case changes
   a decision; when a bounded search finds nothing more, record the gap and move on. For each, record what it needed
   to do, what it did and why its makers say they chose it, citing the pages you opened. Start from
   [case-library](case-library.md).
2. Write what this film's kind and platform ask for and rule out, citing platform documentation.
3. Annotate every reference, the person's or yours: the timestamp, the one property to borrow (a camera behaviour, a
   reveal, a material response, a balance, an ending), what must not transfer, and, where it sharpens the choice, a
   counterexample that does the opposite job. Label each observation as reported by its makers, seen in frames, or
   played by a named person; a property of motion or sound stays unverified until someone has played it. "Like this"
   without a timestamp is an open question. Draw some references from outside motion design: nature, gesture, dance,
   film. Measure each motion reference's pace with `motion_scan.py` (still share, longest still stretch, cuts per
   10 s; [kinetic-type](kinetic-type.md) §4) and record the numbers beside it. Name a look by its cause (halation,
   bleach bypass, halftone, a dolly zoom): the [VFX Atlas](https://atlas.letz.ai/) describes 504 effects and looks
   from 1820 to 2026 with the optical, chemical or digital cause of each, and knowing the cause tells you how to
   rebuild a look honestly in code.
4. Mine the product itself. Ask the owner what they disliked about how things were done before the product, and list
   the product's own material: the sounds it makes, the states it passes through, what it outputs, the limits it
   works within. Draft at least one concept from that list. A concept that would describe a competitor as well is
   replaced.
5. Propose two or three directions from what the study found, each specific enough to picture: who and what is on
   screen, the first image or line, the peak and how it lands, the look and the sound in a phrase each, and the last
   frame. Give each what it would make the viewer notice and feel, its risks, and the gate at which the person
   compares them.

Numbers in the study are facts about their own films, never targets for this one.

Done when `study.md` holds the cited pieces, the platform notes, the annotated references, the product's material
and the proposed directions.

## 5. The studio gates

Studio grade inserts gates into steps 1-9. At each, the person sees something they can judge, presented as in §3, the
decision is recorded with its sentence, and everything provisional is labelled.

| Gate | In step | The person sees | They decide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concept sketches | 2 | each of three concepts as a rough of three moments (the idea, what gets in its way, what happens next), a few seconds each, with temp sound | the concept, and the best idea to keep from the others |
| Styleframes | 4 | stills of the opening, the proof, the ending and the frame with the most text or interface, with approved copy, in every delivered aspect ratio, at full size and at viewing size | the look: colours, type and imagery (motion is judged at the animatic) |
| Animatic | 3-4 | the whole film as timed stills or simple moving drafts, with temp sound and voice | story, order and pacing |
| Sound direction | 5 | two or three treatments that differ in one choice, and music candidates against the animatic, loudness matched | whether music, voice, effects or quiet carry each passage; the music; the sound's one rule |
| Picture lock | 6 | the full cut, muted, at viewing size | the picture and its timing |
| Final | 7-9 | the finished file and its deliverables, muted, then with sound | release |

For a silent deliverable, the sound-direction gate and every "with sound" pass are recorded as not applicable, with
the reason, and with the audio stream verified absent, or verified silent where the destination requires a track.

- **Concept sketches.** Run the operation and decoding tests ([story-and-score](story-and-score.md) §4) on all three
  sketches before the choice, not only on the winner.
- **Styleframes.** Frame the hardest moments, not the hero frame: a dense frame with real copy in a square or vertical
  crop is where a look fails later, at greater cost. Write beside each what stills cannot settle (rhythm, easing, how
  a join reads) and which motion test will settle it.
- **Animatic.** Block key poses first: each scene as held states on the real timeline, joined by cuts or plain linear
  moves. Approve that every state reads and the order holds before adding easing, overlap and secondary motion. Keep
  the blocking render: it is the rough that §8 compares against.
- **Picture lock.** After lock, picture changes reopen the sound gate for the affected passages.

## 6. Spend where it shows

Spend on this film's actual bottleneck, and give every line an expected improvement and a fallback. Product truth is
never where to save: when the product must be exact, its proof is real capture, CAD or deterministic animation. Beyond
that, the priorities depend on the film:

- **Narrated:** the voice performance, the music's fit and an intelligible mix.
- **Product-led:** accurate product work and one coherent hero sequence.
- **Music-led:** a track whose structure follows the story's curve, cleared for the real use, with music stems (its
  parts, such as drums and melody, as separate files).
- **Silent:** picture, type and motion; music and voice budgets go to zero.

Buy each item at the gate that needs it, never before the decision it serves. Use previews and watermarked comps only
where their licence permits, and keep them out of anything shared outside the project. Reserve money for replacing an
asset whose rights fail at clearance. For generated material, set criteria before reviewing takes, work in bounded
batches, and track cost per accepted second of the cut ([generation](generation.md) §4). Money does not fix an
unclear idea: when a moment fails because the concept is unclear, fix the concept first.

## 7. Craft passes

Use these passes the way a specialist would: while making the concept sketches, styleframes, animatic and sound
direction, where they can still change the design, and again at picture lock and before delivery, to verify those
decisions and catch regressions. The techniques are options, not a house style: choose those that serve this film, and
record a pass as not applicable when its subject is absent. Truth, rights, accessibility and delivered-file QC stay
mandatory. These passes add to the main references; they do not repeat them. Record each pass's findings in
`review-log.md` and verify them like any critic's ([review](review.md) §5). A pass that needs perception is complete
only when its named person has made the judgement (§1).

### 7.1 Story and idea

- **Audit the analogy.** Answer yes or no, with a reason, for each way a metaphor misleads: it matches the product in
  appearance but not in operation; it credits the product with agency it lacks; it implies a larger effect than the
  facts support; it hides a prerequisite (setup, data, a person); the film uses more than one model for the same
  thing; a viewer could recall the spectacle without the product. Fix every yes before polishing.
- **Name the analogy's job:** explain an operation, convey a feeling or attach an identity. An analogy that explains
  an operation still needs the product's real input and output on screen, and no factual claim rests on an analogy
  whose job is feeling.
- **When an analogy will not decode, step down before adding words.** Ask whether the viewer recognised the objects,
  then whether they inferred the relation. If they saw the objects but missed the relation, show the two terms side
  by side before merging them, and test again.
- **Keep one question open at a time.** Keep one thing for the viewer to work out by design, and answer it while
  they still care; everything else stays readable. Unreadable text is a defect, not mystery.
- **Make each stretch earn the next.** Add a score column, "why the viewer stays for the next moment". Finishing a
  read, comparing, absorbing a result and anticipating are legitimate jobs. Where a row has none of them and
  attention, understanding and feeling are all unchanged from the row before, cut it or give it a consequence, a
  piece of evidence or a reinterpretation; shorten a hold only after a normal-speed comparison.
- **Make the density curve the argument.** Write it in one line (simple to rich, rich to simple, steady with one
  break) with the claim it expresses. If the claim is simplicity, compare an ending with less competing information
  than the opening.
- **Put the expert's proof inside the newcomer's action.** Place one detail an expert would recognise as hard inside
  the main action. A newcomer's account must be complete without it, and no qualifier lives only in that detail.
- **Make the demonstration falsifiable.** Complete "If the product failed, the viewer would see ___." If you cannot,
  choose a different demonstration. Then check that each cause appears before its effect.

### 7.2 Picture and motion

- **Separate evidence from treatment.** Record each shot's evidence class (recorded, verified reconstruction,
  illustration, future vision) separately from its look. Keep the region that carries evidence clear of effects that
  obscure or mislead (grain, glow, parallax, drift), and give it a stable view when the viewer must inspect it. A
  clean, stable look never turns an illustration into proof ([product-demos](product-demos.md)).
- **Take motion from a real material.** Write the product's relationship as a verb (connects, carries, filters,
  settles). Choose a material that does that verb physically, list how it moves (bends, pours, stacks, unfolds), use
  only those behaviours for transitions, and check against the facts file that the material implies nothing the
  product does not do.
- **One camera rule from the concept.** Write one camera or motion rule that states the concept, apply it to every
  shot, and log each exception with its reason. Shots from different worlds cut together because they share it.
- **Match implied effort to the promise.** Give no heavy landing or long wind-up to an action the film calls
  effortless, and show a constant-rate process at a constant rate. Keep squash and stretch off type, charts and logos
  unless distortion is the point.
- **Point the eye with anticipation.** Before an action the viewer must not miss, add a smaller preparatory move on
  or towards the element that will act; the person confirms at normal speed that their eye arrives first.
- **Separate a path's shape from its speed.** Define a curved move's path first, then drive the distance along it with
  its own timing curve, using an arc-length lookup so the curve's uneven parameter spacing adds no accidental
  acceleration. Plot distance per frame and check the onion frames.
- **Show mass with follow-through.** In a multi-part object, name the driver and the parts that hang off it; give
  dependent parts a small, damped lag after the driver stops, computed from time. Keep text, logos and evidence rigid,
  and let the final reading state settle completely.
- **Light a reflective product through its reflections.** Start from a neutral diagnostic scene; shape curvature,
  bevels and silhouette with broad light cards and dark flags before raising brightness or bloom. Check several
  orientations and the whole camera move for vanishing edges and clipped highlights, and never alter measured
  geometry to improve a highlight.
- **Material, weight and speed are judged in motion** by the person: highlights must travel with the object, and a
  take whose speed looks wrong is rejected even when every frame looks right.
- **Charts:** lock axes, scale and viewpoint while values change. Reveal measured values rather than counting through
  values nobody measured ([claims-and-rights](claims-and-rights.md) §2); interpolate only where the interpolation is
  meaningful and shown as such. Eased geometry never overshoots, and the chart ends on a held state where every value
  to compare is visible at once.
- **Typed text:** compute each character's or word's reveal time on the shared clock and quantise it to frames, so
  the average rate holds without drift even when intervals alternate. Declare the typing range narrowly to
  `motion_scan.py --ignore`, and still inspect every flag inside it: a declared range can hide an unrelated defect.
- **Type settling into focus** gets blur scaled to its size, cleared while it is still moving. Inspect where blurred
  type meets a mask edge: a hard clip through the blur reads as a smudge.

### 7.3 Type

- **Give every string a role:** identity, headline, explanation, interface evidence, qualifier or texture. Roles the
  viewer must read get reading windows; each qualifier stays with its claim. Identity and texture may behave
  expressively; anything carrying proof gets a held, fully readable state.
- **Judge strokes, not only contrast.** Contrast ratios miss thin strokes. At the delivered viewing size, check
  stroke weight and the x-height after camera scale and downscaling; where strokes break up, add weight or size, or
  cut words.
- **Proof hero type optically.** Render the actual headline and name strings with kerning and shaping on, and check
  awkward pairs, punctuation, apparent centring, alignment with icons, and the optical-size axis where the font has
  one. Store any correction with its string, never as a global tracking change, and check every language separately.
- **Group with enclosure** where proximity and alignment are not enough to make elements read as a set. When the
  layout rearranges, carry the enclosure with its members or show it releasing them, and remove a connector on the
  frame its relationship stops being true.

### 7.4 Editing and joins

- **At a cut, rank emotion, then story, then rhythm, then continuity**, after truth: a cut may never misrepresent the
  product's state, version, inputs, causality or the elapsed time of a real operation. A true change of state reads
  as one, and compressed time is labelled ([product-demos](product-demos.md) §3). Within that, choose the cut point
  that serves the feeling, and accept a mismatch in composition, gesture or screen direction if that is the cost.
  After a heavy moment, try a version with more room before the next idea.
- **Map where the eye is at each cut.** Mark the focal region in the last readable outgoing frame and the first
  incoming frame. In a continuous argument, put the next important detail near the last one or lead the eye there;
  when a big jump is intended, leave time to find the new place before anything essential happens. Export the paired
  frames with their regions marked for every rendition, and ask a first-time viewer where they had to search.
- **Judge pace by new content per cut, not the number of cuts.** For a passage that feels rushed, list for each cut
  the new concept, the new place to look and the new text it brings. Simplify cuts that bring more than one before
  removing cuts, and introduce no new term while the camera makes an unrelated move.
- **Recurring devices pass a written rule.** Write the rule first ("each pair must ___"), make several times more
  candidates than the film needs, keep only those that pass, and record why each rejected one failed. The person sees
  the shortlist, not the pile.

### 7.5 Sound design

- **One sound rule carries the claim.** After the sound proposition ([sound](sound.md)), state one rule the whole
  soundtrack obeys (a single sustained tone, a short list of named sources, one kind of performer, a deliberate
  absence) and how it states the product's claim. Rework any cue that breaks it without a written reason; the person
  judges whether the rule still holds attention in the final third.
- **Write what sound adds.** For each passage complete "With sound, the viewer also understands or feels ___", and
  prepare it muted, as sound only, and combined. If the sentence is not true of the combined version, change how
  sound relates to picture, not its level.
- **Build the pressure the reveal relieves.** Design the before and after as one pair. For relief, let the before
  carry the burden and the reveal remove it; offer a continuing ambience or tail, a deliberate dry stop and silence as
  alternatives, and keep whichever the person chooses, free of edge clicks. For continuity, carry one sound across the
  change. For precision, keep one distinct sound unused until the action that proves the claim.
- **Set up the payoffs.** For each reveal, decide whether an earlier sound sets up the expectation, and whether the
  reveal fulfils, delays or breaks it. Put no riser before every transition, and keep builds out of reading passages.
- **Refuse the category's default when it lies.** Write the category's default sound and choose to use it or refuse
  it, with the reason. A glitch, stutter or alarm-like cue taken literally can imply a failure the product does not
  have.
- **Give the sound a point of view:** close and bodily, inside a mind, outside a mechanism, or performance. Write what
  it supplies that the frame does not show, without pulling the eye from the subject.
- **Let perspective follow the move.** Where the camera or an object changes distance meaningfully, automate level,
  the dry-to-wet balance, high-frequency loss and a restrained pan together as one gesture, and keep the room
  consistent within a space. Decide whether each interface cue belongs to the interface or is an accent outside the
  space; do not sweep the whole soundtrack with every camera move.
- **Play from the middle; play loops on repeat.** The person starts playback after the opening and notes any sound
  whose meaning depends on something earlier, and plays a loop three or more times to catch a cue that becomes
  intrusive.
- **In data pieces, sound points or encodes.** A pointing cue implies no direction or count. An encoding keeps the
  data's order, has its mapping written down, and matches the chart on screen.
- **Caption meaningful non-speech sound.** If removing a cue changes what the viewer knows, show that information in
  the picture or caption it.

### 7.6 Music

- **Hold the music back so its entrance is the release.** For an action-led film, compare a version where effects
  carry the opening and the music first enters at the payoff against continuous music on the same cut.
- **Keep a finished song from turning the film into a music video.** When the film turns several times, test track
  fragments that change at each turn, with one element (a pitch, a rhythm, an instrument) running throughout and the
  full theme only at the close.
- **Separate "too active" from "the wrong mood".** For too fast or busy, change density, tempo or subdivision and
  keep harmony and instruments. For the wrong feeling, change harmony, instruments or timbre and keep the activity.
- **Shape music and picture in both directions.** Describe the music's arc in words before locking shot timings, get
  a draft that follows it, then move picture events to the phrases where a turn lands better, where the reading
  windows and the product's real timing still allow.
- **Edit music by harmony and waveform, not only bar numbers.** For every splice, note the chord and bass on each side,
  the phrase's function and where the transients fall. Try a straight cut at a compatible boundary before an overlap,
  carry a cymbal or reverb tail on its own layer when cutting the phrase under it, compare crossfade curves at matched
  level, and never stack two kick attacks. The person listens to each join in the full cut.
- **Keep an existing sonic identity's core.** List the features that make the brand's sonic logo recognisable, keep
  them, adapt the length to the cut and bring it in from the film's own palette. Present a new sonic logo as a
  proposal to test over repeated viewings.

### 7.7 Voice

- **Cast before you polish the script.** The person compares candidate voices on the same passage, including the
  hardest name and the emotional turn, at matched loudness.
- **Keep a designed voice as a saved voice.** Save a synthetic voice you designed through the vendor's persistent
  voice feature, record its voice ID, model and settings, and reuse that identity for every line. Cloning from a
  recording is a different operation: use it only where the route supports it, the speaker has consented and the
  rights allow it.
- **Record pickups in context.** Generate or record each pickup with the phrases before and after it, compare complete
  phrases rather than stitching fragments, and keep a take sheet of pronunciation, stressed word, breath and tempo.
  Reconcile every final line with the approved script and captions.
- **Edit the voice before mastering it.** Keep the dry original. Fix clicks, plosives, harsh sibilance, room changes
  and uneven phrases with clip gain and local repair first, then targeted de-essing, before any compression; keep
  consonant starts, word endings and the breaths that carry meaning. Compare treated and untreated at matched
  loudness before adopting the chain.
- **Direct for meaning.** Mark each line's operative word and intent ("reporting", "reassuring", "inviting"), and
  deliver serious facts as clear reporting rather than lament.

### 7.8 Mix

- **One sound in front at each moment.** For each busy passage, list the elements by kind (melodic, rhythmic,
  textural, voice, effects) and decide which has the listener's attention at each moment. Where elements compete for
  the same time and frequency range, compare simplifying the arrangement, moving a register, changing an envelope or
  tail, and local automation, before touching overall levels. Two independent lines can be the arrangement; keep them
  when they stay intelligible and serve the idea.
- **Lift a quiet passage only when a listener finds it hard to follow.** Then use plain gain, ramped to the phrase or
  event the passage follows, keep any contrast the story depends on, and recheck true peak after the re-encode.
- **Listening on the devices that matter belongs to the person:** phone speaker, laptop and headphones, and mono.
  Prepare the versions at a documented playback level; where a platform's normalisation is unverified, record it as
  unverified rather than inventing a target.

### 7.9 Finishing

- **Interpret each source before matching it.** For camera log, HDR, display-referred stock, browser renders and
  generated EXR, record each source's primaries, transfer function and range, choose one working space and one output
  space, and apply each input transform and the output transform once. Mark an unknown source as unresolved rather
  than guessing from a washed-out preview, and route exact interface and brand graphics on their own documented path.
- **Then match with scopes.** Export waveform, RGB parade and vectorscope views for representative frames of every
  source and at every shot boundary, and fix clipping, crushed detail, casts and large jumps before applying one
  restrained shared look. Keep brand colours, status colours and evidence legible through that look. A named person
  approves the match on the actual encode; scopes show the signal, not the taste.
- **Stress-test the platform encode.** Re-encode the delivered file the way platforms commonly serve it (for example
  1280x720 H.264 at about 2 Mb/s, with the settings recorded) and inspect dark gradients, glows and the smallest
  essential text at full size and at viewing size. Grain fine enough to hide banding in the master is often stripped
  by such an encode; a coarser texture can survive it, so test both on the film's worst frames. Report this as a
  stress test, not as the rendition every viewer receives, and avoid dot or line patterns near pixel pitch.
- **Treat alpha deliberately.** Record whether each plate is straight or premultiplied, use the matching compositing
  mode, unpremultiply only for operations that need straight colour, and extend edge colour before filtering. Check
  semi-transparent shadows, hair and glows over black, white and a saturated ground at final scale, in motion.
- **Keep precision, chroma and archive purpose separate.** State where the master's precision comes from: a browser
  capture is usually 8-bit, and writing it into a 10-bit codec recovers nothing. Where coloured edges matter, keep a
  lossless RGB or 4:4:4 source; when converting it, try
  `scale=out_color_matrix=bt709:out_range=tv:flags=lanczos+accurate_rnd+full_chroma_int+full_chroma_inp` and compare
  saturated edges against the standard route at 2-4x zoom, then tag and verify as in [finishing](finishing.md) §3.
  Measure the bytes per frame on a short window before committing disk.
- **Supersample only what aliases.** Render at twice the scale and downscale with a filtered scaler only where a 1:1
  crop shows aliasing, typically interface or type under perspective.

### 7.10 Product demonstrations

- **Show the real product where the film makes a claim** ([product-demos](product-demos.md)), at a scale where its
  interface reads at viewing size, with plausible invented data.
- **Let the product's real timing stand.** When an operation takes time, keep the measured wait, or label any
  compression or illustrative timing ([product-demos](product-demos.md) §3); never shorten an apparent wait silently.

## 8. The studio loop and the bar

The main method stops when no blocking or visible finding remains. Studio grade keeps going until the film clears the
bar, and the bar never overrides a hard limit, a truth rule or a failed check.

**The bar.** At the brief, write for each question below the response that would show it is met, such as what a
viewer should be able to say back. After each round, record for each question the named viewer (ideally someone from
the intended audience who has not seen earlier cuts), the exact version, how they watched and their own words before
any prompting, and, kept separate, the approver's decision: met, not met or unknown. Never combine the decisions into
a score: averaging lets polish hide an empty middle or an unearned payoff.

- **Understanding.** Can the intended viewer rebuild the change and the product's role?
- **Specificity.** Does the action reveal something only this product does?
- **Experience.** Is the response worth having, beyond admiring the effort?
- **Earned payoff.** Does the culmination depend on what was planted?
- **Truth.** Does the picture support what viewers will infer?
- **Afterimage.** Does the viewer's unprompted recall hold the intended idea, attached to the right product? Record
  whether that was immediate recall or after a delay; one viewer's memory is not an audience's.

"Not met" and "unknown" are open work, even when every QC check passes. You cannot answer these for a viewer: without
one, the decision stays unknown.

**Each round:**

1. Record the six decisions with their evidence.
2. Name the weakest three seconds and the weakest channel in them (picture, motion, sound, words or product truth).
3. Make two or three alternatives for that moment, each from a different starting point, with its entry, exit and
   claims fixed.
4. Show them to the person in motion and, unless the film is silent, with sound.
5. Build the choice, then run QC and the change proof ([revisions](../SKILL.md#revisions)).
6. Compare the polished moments with the approved rough at the same size. Polish adds texture, light and secondary
   motion, which can bury the one relationship the idea depends on; when it has, remove or recompose the added detail.
7. Put the film beside the agreed references, at the same size and in the same conditions, and name what still
   separates them. Sitting beside a reference is a comparison, not proof of equal quality.

**Stop** when every question is met with evidence and a named person has watched (and, unless silent, listened), or
when the person decides to stop. A person's decision to stop is recorded as theirs; it does not mean the bar was
cleared. When you stop, list what you would still raise, and why. An empty defect list is never the reason to stop.

## 9. Deliver at studio grade

Deliver what was agreed in §2, on top of the main deliverables ([formats](formats.md)), each with its own check:

- **Master:** a high-quality intermediate from the same frozen render (for example ProRes 422 HQ, or 4444 when alpha
  or coloured-edge detail must survive), its precision and chroma stated honestly (§7.9). Check it with `qc_file.py`
  against its own spec (for example `--vcodec prores --pix-fmt yuv422p10le --color bt709 --frames N`), confirm the
  range in the `--json` report, and leave out web-only checks such as `--faststart`.
- **Mix stems:** dialogue, music and effects from the same timeline start, sample rate, sample count and channel
  layout, with their automation, delays and effect returns, and tails inside the duration. Say whether they
  reconstruct the approved mix, and test it: sum them at unity and subtract the mix before its encode. An exact
  reconstruction leaves a residual peak of `-inf` or only rounding noise; FFmpeg exits 0 either way, so read the
  peak. For stereo stems:

  ```
  ffmpeg -i dialogue.wav -i music.wav -i effects.wav -i mix.wav -filter_complex \
    "[0][1][2]amix=inputs=3:normalize=0[s];[s][3]amerge=inputs=2,pan=stereo|c0=c0-c2|c1=c1-c3,astats=measure_perchannel=none:measure_overall=Peak_level" \
    -f null -
  ```

  When shared bus processing prevents an exact sum, document that and supply the processing recipe. Never normalise
  each stem separately.
- **Localisation material, when versions in other languages are in scope:** a textless picture from the same render,
  editable copy and captions, a full music-and-effects mix with the gaps under dialogue filled, handles on shots
  likely to be retimed, and a list of any wording baked into the picture. Check that the textless picture matches the
  approved one in frame count, cut positions and colour, with no surviving titles and no missing graphics; a named
  listener checks the music-and-effects mix for residual speech and holes; record each handle's length.
- **Cut-downs and renditions:** each composed for its own length and aspect ratio, never trimmed blind from the
  master.
- **Captions** as sidecar files, and burned in where a rendition requires or the person agrees it, never doubled with
  a platform's own captions. Check that cues sit inside the duration, never overlap by accident, and match the
  approved wording.
- **Poster frames and thumbnails** chosen on purpose and checked at their displayed size.
- **The archive, in two parts.** The maker's retained archive holds the project, frozen sources, the asset register
  with every licence and receipt, `budget.csv` with actual spend, the study, the decision sentences and the delivery
  report. The client's handover contains only what the licences allow to be passed on; list every excluded
  dependency (fonts, stock originals, library sounds) with its version and how to licence it again.

Done when every agreed deliverable passes its own checks, and the delivery report states the bar's six decisions
with their evidence.
