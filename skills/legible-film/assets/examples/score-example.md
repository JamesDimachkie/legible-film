# A worked score: a fictional 40-second launch

A score is a timeline of what the audience sees and hears at each moment. This one is filled in for an invented
product, to show what "a job, not an adjective" means in every channel, including the cells deliberately left quiet.
Copy its method, never its timings, motif or proportions: replace every capability, time and sound with your own
film's, and read "How to challenge this draft" before using it.

If you commissioned the film rather than making it: your agent fills this in. You react to the proposed story and the
examples it shows you; the grid is its production plan.

**Product (fictional).** Larkspool is a review workspace. Assume it lets two reviewers work on the same version, keeps
their comments and individual approvals, and lets a person publish an approved snapshot with its history. It does not
decide correctness, guarantee consensus, resolve conflicts by itself, or imply any particular speed.

- **The change in the viewer:** a team that could not tell which version was approved can now point to one, with its
  approvals attached.
- **Governing idea:** separate contributions become one usable decision without losing who made them.
- **Peak:** a snapshot a person chose to publish becomes inspectable, with both approvals beside it.
- **Motif** (an idea that recurs): two short, distinct lines and two related musical figures (short phrases), first
  competing for attention, later heard as compatible parts. They keep their identities when they converge.
- **No voice.** Every essential line is on screen.

In `score.csv` each moment is a row; here the channels run down the side so the eight moments read across.

**Critical timing decisions.** Three events are shared by picture and sound: P1, the shared object arrives (about
11 s); P2, the person presses Publish (late in 25-28 s); P3, the published state appears (about 28 s). The music's
settling arrival and the confirmation sound belong to P3, not P2: the release comes when the product's state is
real, not when the button is pressed. If the real operation takes longer, move P3 and reshape the phrase; never
shorten the wait by quietly retiming the operation. A schematic animation may use designed timing only if it is
labelled as one.

**Why six seconds after the peak.** The result carries several pieces of evidence (the version, two approvals, the
history), so the draft reserves time to inspect it and delays the end card. Six seconds is a design allocation, not a
reading threshold; viewing at phone size and normal speed may call for fewer words, a closer view or a longer hold.

**How to challenge this draft.**

- If the audience already understands version control, spend less time on the copies and more on the hard decision.
- If the product cannot show an approval history, remove that claim and redesign the governing idea.
- If the converging lines suggest automatic consensus, show the two people's actions more explicitly, or drop the
  analogy.
- If viewers watching muted miss the published state, change the picture before making the confirmation louder.
