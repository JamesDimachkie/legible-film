# Story and score

From a product and its context to one story, and from the story to a score: every moment planned across picture,
motion, text, music, effects, voice and the product's truth. Use it in step 2, and again whenever a cut is busy
and nobody can say what it is about.

The bar is one legible experience: the problem, the solution and the presentation work as a single story curve,
carried by picture, sound, cues and the product itself, for the audience that will watch it. "Good" and
"professional" are where this work starts, not where it stops.

## 1. Facts that decide the story

Collect these before any design work, and keep the unknowns visible in the brief:

- **The owner's own words:** launch posts, site copy, docs, the way they describe the product to a friend. A story
  that follows them is easier to approve; a story that reorders them for the viewer's sake is often stronger.
- **The brand as served:** colour tokens, logo files and fonts captured from the product's own site and CSS, not
  from screenshots.
- **The audience:** who must understand this, what they already know about the problem, the category and the
  alternatives, and what would make them doubt the result.
- **The task that changes:** what happens without the product, what input, action and result can be shown
  honestly, and what still needs a person, a prerequisite or a qualification.
- **The job:** explain a capability, make it desirable, overturn a belief, or prompt an action.
- **The medium:** where it plays, at what size, and whether it must work muted (a feed autoplays muted on a phone).

Write the audience and its most likely misunderstanding in one sentence each. Makers cannot easily imagine not
knowing what they know (the curse of knowledge), so the newcomer's reading is tested, never assumed.

## 2. The change, its evidence and its boundary

Fill in all three before choosing a look:

- **The change:** "They begin believing ___. They finish understanding or feeling ___ because they see ___."
- **The mechanism:** "When ___ happens, ___ becomes possible, and we can see that because ___."
- **The boundary:** "This does not establish ___." Keep it beside the concept, where it can veto motion, sound or
  copy that would imply more.

A draft whose evidence is only an adjective ("effortless", "powerful", "cinematic") has no story yet. If the
product is about desire or identity, name the experience that becomes available instead of inventing a pain.

Lead with what is genuinely impressive about the product. Limitations are not an emotional hook; the qualifiers
that change a claim's meaning stay readable beside the claim, and the story is built on the strength.

## 3. Three concepts, one operation

Write three materially different concepts before choosing:

1. **The product in use:** the real task produces the result on screen.
2. **A relational analogy:** a familiar action makes the product's important relation visible. Map it as
   *familiar relation → product relation → visible evidence → false implication*. An analogy carries a relation,
   not a resemblance: a lock can imply complete protection, a conductor an authority the product lacks.
3. **A reframe:** later evidence changes how the viewer reads the beginning.

For each, write the opening question, the product-caused event, the proof, the peak, the final meaning and the
most likely misunderstanding. Start from what this product does beyond the obvious version (how it resolves a
conflict, ranks a result, changes only the relevant records), never from a style.

Then state the chosen concept as a governing operation, a verb with consequences rather than a look:

> Because the product does ___, the film's ___ changes from ___ to ___. We recognise it again when ___.
> The analogy stops at ___.

Note what stays stable through the film (a task, a region of the interface, a silhouette, a coordinate frame) and
what changes (the result, scale, arrangement, material, complexity). A film that keeps one reference stable while
everything around it develops is easy to follow.

## 4. Four tests before anything expensive

Run each on the chosen concept and write the answer, with its reason, into `treatment.md`:

| Test | How | It fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **Operation** | Make the smallest change that embodies the idea (one hand-off, one release) and look at it. | It needs explaining to read. |
| **Truth** | Beside each transformation, write the real feature, or the stated illustration it stands for, and where the analogy stops. | A transformation implies a capability, relation or measurement the facts file lacks. |
| **Decoding** | Show it once at normal speed to someone unfamiliar; ask what changed, what caused it and what the product did, before explaining. | Their answer differs from the change sentence. |
| **Specificity** | Put a competitor's name where the product's is, and ask whether the same demonstration also describes a simpler feature. | The film still makes the same claim: it shows a category, not this product. |

Add the muted test for anything that plays in a feed: with the sound off, can a viewer follow cause, action and
result, and say whose product it is?

## 5. Structure, turn, peak and ending

| Starting situation | Structure to try first |
|---|---|
| The obstacle is unfamiliar | problem → solution → visible proof → consequence |
| The audience already wants the task solved | a demonstration that builds, or a comparable before and after |
| People misjudge a familiar behaviour or product | setup → later evidence → reinterpretation |
| The value is a new relation with no useful antagonist | introduction → development → turn → connection |
| One rule has delightful consequences | establish the rule → develop its consequences → culmination |

- **The turn** changes the viewer's model of the situation. A bigger headline is not a turn.
- **The peak** is the strongest felt event: "At the peak, the viewer can finally understand ___ because they see
  ___." Prepare it (teach an operation or a question), develop it (vary it so the next consequence is
  anticipated), withhold one consequential part, then release it with evidence the viewer can inspect.
- Save one dimension of change for the peak. Maximising scale, brightness, motion and impacts everywhere leaves
  nothing to arrive.
- **The ending** has its own job: an action, a final proof state, an afterimage, the opening returning with new
  meaning, or a loop. Write the peak and the ending separately.
- A late reveal answers a question the viewer already holds; it expands the promise rather than starting a
  second story.
- Starting drafts, not rules: in 15 s the result lands around 8-11 s, in 30 s the payoff around 20-25 s, in 60 s
  around 43-51 s, each leaving time for the consequence to register. When the proof needs longer, shorten the
  setup or narrow the promise. Density sets duration: lengthen a piece before cramming a scene, and when the
  duration is fixed, cut a task.

What research supports, with its limits: a 2022 meta-analysis (Alaybek et al., 174 independent samples) found
remembered evaluations track the peak and the end strongly, while the overall average predicts them about as well,
so the middle cannot be neglected. Keltner and Haidt propose that awe centres on perceived vastness plus a need to
revise one's understanding; a big object with dramatic music supplies neither. "Surprise, then inevitability" is a
craft test: after the reveal, do the earlier details make sense?

## 6. The thread the viewer follows

When the viewer is meant to follow one element continuously (a line, a card, a record, a cursor, the product's
own mark), that element is the film's thread. It behaves the same everywhere: it keeps going in the direction it
was going and leads into the next thing. It never stalls then darts, never blinks out to be replaced by a
look-alike from the frame edge, and never connects two things whose relation the facts do not support.

- Choose an element the product owns: its record, its state, its mark. A generic dot or block says nothing about
  this product.
- Give it a state that changes along the way, so that following it is following the story.
- Not every film needs one. A cut-driven explainer or an interview carries its continuity in voice and argument.

## 7. The score

Lay the film out as a score before building: one moment per row, with a column for each channel. The template is
[score.csv](../assets/templates/score.csv).

| Column | Write |
|---|---|
| Story beat | what changes, and what question stays open |
| Picture | the object or state the viewer can inspect |
| Motion | its cause, path, persistence and stopping condition |
| Text | the exact words, and when they are fully readable |
| Music | the phrase's job and how it changes |
| Effects, voice or quiet | the event or viewpoint each cue carries, including deliberate absence |
| Product truth | the capability shown, its evidence, its boundary, recorded or illustrative |
| Understanding | the sentence a newcomer could say afterwards |
| Feeling | the intended change, a hypothesis until someone watches |
| Lead and anchor | which channel leads, what must coincide, what may drift |

Fill story, truth and understanding first, then decide what each channel adds; leave a channel's cell empty when
it adds nothing. Give the decisive events IDs that picture and sound share ("the confirmation sound's attack coincides
with `published-visible`", not "sound at 28 s"); those IDs become the cue list in [timing](timing.md).

Musical structure applied to story helps: a **motif** (a small recognisable action or contour), **variation** (the
same operation under a changed condition), **development** (it meets a constraint and must change),
**recapitulation** (the opening returns with changed meaning). Relate the channels on purpose: precise coincidence
makes a state change unmistakable; the same trajectory in another material gives abstract motion a felt character;
sound before the image creates expectation; withholding makes a return count. Tight mapping is not automatically
weak, and counterpoint is not automatically deep; each must add meaning.

## 8. The product as instrument

Say which kind of truth each element carries: **recorded product material** (real interface, real output, real
device sounds), **derived from data by a stated mapping** (a chart, a sonification), or **designed metaphor**.
Never present a designed flourish as measured data. A software product's own behaviour can become the motif when
its boundary holds:

| Material | Motion and sound idea | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| State change | a stable object changes contour; a phrase changes its ending | completion cues only for real completion |
| Data flow | marks follow valid routes; timbres keep their origin across a hand-off | no implied integration that does not exist |
| Latency | an unresolved gesture holds through the wait; sound opens on the result | measured waits keep real time; a schematic says so |
| Scale | a learned operation repeats and groups into phrases | density is not a throughput claim |
| Error | the established pattern breaks; the unresolved state shows | no implication that every error is caught |
| Collaboration | separate agents keep their colours and paths on one object | no implied consensus or merged authorship |

## 9. Words

On-screen text, narration, captions and the post that carries the film are one script, held to the claim rules in
[claims-and-rights](claims-and-rights.md). Lead with the strongest true thing; let the picture say what it can and
cut the words that repeat it; keep every condition beside the claim it limits. Use the viewer's own words for the
problem. A phrase from the opening can come back at the end with its meaning changed by what the film showed
(recapitulation, in the score's terms), provided the picture makes the new meaning true.

## 10. Test legibility before polish

Show the rough sequence once, muted, at the real viewing size, to people who have not heard the pitch; keep experts
and newcomers apart. Ask, in their own words: what happened and what changed; what caused it and what the product
does and does not do; where they were first unsure; what stays most vivid, and with which product; what they would
need to believe it. Diagnose missed information, wrong mapping, disbelief and indifference separately, fix the
cause, and retest with fresh viewers. Synthetic critics can catch decoding problems in a script and in frames
([review](review.md)); they do not replace a person watching at speed.

## 11. Hand-off to building

`treatment.md` ([template](../assets/templates/treatment.md)) carries: the audience, the change with its evidence
and boundary, the three concepts and why one won, the governing operation, the test answers with their reasons, the
structure, the thread if any, the score, the exact peak event, the ending's job, the script, the cover frame, the
assumptions you made, and the open questions for viewers. Then decide the clock ([timing](timing.md)), the picture
([picture](picture.md)) and the sound ([sound](sound.md)).
